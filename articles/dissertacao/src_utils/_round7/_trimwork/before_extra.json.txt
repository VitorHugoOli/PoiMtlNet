About this volume
This volume holds two appendices that were written for the dissertation “From Represen￾tations to a Single Joint Model: Multi-Task Learning for Point-of-Interest Category and Region
Prediction” and that are published beside it rather than inside it. The first records every departure
of the three reproduced article chapters from their published or submitted originals. The second
measures how much of the next category can be predicted from the category history of the input
window alone, which is the reference level one screening argument in the dissertation is read
against.
Both were part of the main document until July of 2026. They were moved here on the
advisor’s assessment that a reproduced chapter need not be an identical copy of the article it
reproduces, and that the short preface opening each of those chapters already tells the reader
what was changed and why. That assessment settles the question the errata appendix was written
to answer, at least for the reader of the dissertation. It leaves the underlying record worth keeping,
because a departure from a published text should be documented somewhere a reader can reach,
and this volume is where it is documented.
The dissertation is complete without this volume. Nothing here is required to follow
its argument, to read any of its results, or to judge the work: every claim the dissertation
makes is stated and supported in the dissertation itself. What this volume adds is auditability. A
reader comparing a reproduced chapter against the article it came from will find the differences
enumerated here instead of having to reconstruct them, and a reader who wants the fuller
treatment of the label-history benchmark will find it here rather than in the paragraph that
summarizes it.
Two conventions follow from the split. The two appendices keep the letters they carried
in the main document, B and D, so that a reference to “Appendix B” means the same thing in both
volumes and in the correspondence around them; the letters A, C and E belong to appendices
that stayed in the dissertation and are not reproduced here. And a reference in these pages to
a numbered chapter, section, table or figure is a reference into the dissertation, not into this
volume: this volume numbers only its own two appendices and their tables.Appendix5

B.4 Two extractions of the same Gowalla corpus
Chapters 3 and 4 report the Florida subset of Gowalla as 20,301 users, 65,009 POIs, and
990,518 check-ins, while Chapter 5 reports 21,052 users, 76,544 POIs, and 1,407,034 check-ins
for the same state of the same public dataset. The two are different extractions of that state, not
different datasets and not a discrepancy between them: the first two chapters report the corpus
as the extraction pipeline of their time produced it, and Chapter 5 reports the current extraction.
The mechanism is the category mapping, not the raw data and not a different filtering rule.
The extraction assigns a category to each check-in from a mapping table and discards the rows
whose category does not map, so widening that table keeps rows that an earlier run had dropped.
The mapping files were extended about eleven months after the earlier Florida extraction, and the
places they added fall mostly in Entertainment, Outdoors, and Travel. A controlled comparison
against the working extraction of the earlier period confirms the direction: each of its POIs,
users, and check-ins reappears in the current extraction, which adds further ones. The current
pipeline therefore cannot reproduce the earlier counts, and the two sets of figures are kept as
each chapter reported them rather than retrofitted to one another. The comparison is recorded in
the research repository, in src_utils/cbic_recompute_result.md.
B.5 The scope of the static task in Chapter 4
Chapter 4 reports two tasks. One is sequential: predict the category of the next place a
user visits, from the history of that user’s check-ins. The other is static: classify the category
of a place from that place’s own representation. This section records what we established afterAPPENDIX B. Errata to the Reproduced Articles 13
publication about the second one, and it bears on how that chapter’s category results should be
read.
The place embedding that Chapter 4 feeds to the static task is built from a fine-grained
class label attached to each place, and that label determines the target category exactly. Measured
over the five Gowalla state subsets the collection uses, the fine class takes between 284 and 365
distinct values per state, every one of them maps to exactly one of the seven top-level categories,
and none maps to more than one. The embedding is a lookup on the fine class, and the fine class
is a strict refinement of the label. So the static task’s input determines its target by construction,
and the reported accuracy on that task measures a deterministic mapping rather than a learned
semantic inference.
Three qualifications matter, and none of them is a softening.
The first is that the sequential task is unaffected. Its input is a history of visits and its target
is a category the model has not seen for that step, so no equivalent identity exists between input
and label. Every claim Chapter 4 makes about the sequential task, including the improvement
that the decomposed representation produces, stands as published.
The second is that the two chapters differ in how direct the channel is, and the difference
is one of degree rather than of kind. Chapter 3 builds each node’s input feature from the average
of that place’s neighbors’ one-hot categories, excluding the place itself, so no place’s own label
appears in its own input. That exclusion does not survive the graph convolution: the place graph
is undirected, so a place appears in its neighbors’ features, and one convolution aggregates over
the neighborhood together with the place itself. A place’s own label therefore returns to its own
embedding at the first hop, averaged in with the neighborhood. So the earlier chapter’s static
task also has a path from label to representation. It is indirect and averaged, where Chapter 4’s
is an exact lookup, and that distinction is the whole of the difference between them.
The third is that Chapter 5 does not inherit the problem, for the reason that chapter gives
on its own terms: it replaces the place-level embedding with a check-in-level representation,
whose input is a single visit, and the identity described above does not arise.
This appendix, and not the chapter, is where the statement belongs, because Chapter 4
is a published, co-authored article and this appendix is the instrument this collection uses for
the difference between what the published text says and what we know now. The measurement
is recorded in the repository released with this work.

APPENDIX D – A Label-History
Benchmark for the Next-Category Task
This appendix answers one question. How much of the next category can be predicted
from the category history of the input window alone, without reading any representation at all?
The answer fixes how strong a screening argument in Chapter 5 can honestly be.
The screening procedure works as follows. For each candidate representation, a linear
model is trained to predict the next category from the vector of the last visit in the window
alone, and the macro-F1 it reaches is compared against the score a reference encoder reaches on
the same task. A candidate scoring far above that reference encoder is suspected of carrying the
label it is supposed to predict, and is discarded. The probe is run twice, once on standardized
features and once on raw ones, and either run can disqualify a candidate.
All scores in this appendix are macro-F1 on a zero-to-one scale, and differences between
them are quoted in points, where one point is 0.01 on that scale.
Two quantities are involved in that comparison. Both have served as the screen’s reference
level at different times, and both have been called the ceiling, which is the confusion this appendix
exists to remove. Each is given one name here and keeps it.
The label-history benchmark. The best macro-F1 obtained from the recorded category history
of the input window alone. No representation is read and no encoder is involved, so this is
a property of the sequence of category labels. It is what this appendix measures.
The clean reference encoder. The encoder that a screened candidate is actually compared
against, and the score the screening margin is applied to. Two encoders play this role
at Florida, both from the lineage the representation of Chapter 5 descends from: one at
0.4090 macro-F1 on standardized features and 0.4074 on raw ones, and a residual variant
at 0.4197 and 0.4182. Where the two are discussed together below, they are named in the
plural for that reason.
The benchmark is measured under four predictors that see category labels and nothing
else. The first is persistence, which repeats the last visited category and fits no parameters. The
second is a multinomial logistic model on a one-hot encoding of that same last category. The
third is the same model on the counts of each category over the nine visits of the window.
The fourth is the same model on positional one-hot encodings of all nine slots, and it is the
only one of the four that can use the order in which the categories occurred. The benchmark
is the best score of the four. All of them run under the protocol the screen itself uses: five-foldAPPENDIX D. A Label-History Benchmark for the Next-Category Task 17
cross-validation grouped by user, so that no user’s windows fall on both sides of a split [2, 3],
with macro-F1 averaged over the five folds. Macro-F1 counts the seven categories equally [4],
which is also why the three logistic models are fitted with balanced class weights: an unweighted
fit optimizes a different objective and would understate the benchmark.
Across the five datasets where the inputs were available the benchmark ranges from
0.2800 to 0.3617, well above the majority-class floor in every case. Table 6 gives the four
predictors, the benchmark, and the floor for each dataset.

The screening comparison is unaffected. Its verdicts are relative, since a candidate is
discarded when it clears the clean reference encoder by more than the screening margin of three
points, and the benchmark enters that arithmetic nowhere. The attention-based encoder that was

The absolute reading is the weaker one. At Florida the two clean reference encoders
score above the benchmark, 0.4090 and 0.4197 against 0.3617, which Chapter 5 reports as a gap
of four to six points, and so does every candidate screened beside them on the raw run of the
probe. One candidate falls below the benchmark on the standardized run, a relation-typed graph
encoder at 0.3328 that rises to 0.4142 on the raw one. It is not an example of a clean encoder
sitting below the benchmark, because it is the candidate that passed this screen and then leaked
under a downstream sequence model, which Chapter 5 reports as the reason the linear form is
a screen rather than a proof. What the screen bounds is therefore the candidates against each
other, not any of them against an absolute standard. That is the weaker of the two claims, and it
is the one Chapter 5 makes.APPENDIX D. A Label-History Benchmark for the Next-Category Task 18
The gap is not by itself evidence of a leak. A per-visit vector legitimately carries more
than the previous category. It also carries the place, the neighborhood of that place in the graph,
and the hour of the visit, and any of those can predict the next category with no information
flowing backward in time.
The benchmark is also not an upper bound. It is the best score of four named predictors,
so a different model reading the same category history could score above it. Calling it a ceiling,
as the internal screening record does, would assert more than the measurement supports.
Two coverage limits apply. Texas is absent because the check-in graph the computation
reads was not retained for that dataset under the shipped representation. Only its embedding
outputs remain, and measuring the benchmark there would require re-running the preprocessing.
For Istanbul, 196 of the 29,816 places carry more than one category across the record. The reason
is not recorded in the data, so none is claimed here.
The table maps each place to its most frequent category. A strict variant that instead
drops every window ending at one of those places keeps 55,946 of the 58,075 windows and
gives 0.3009, against the 0.3016 of the table.
