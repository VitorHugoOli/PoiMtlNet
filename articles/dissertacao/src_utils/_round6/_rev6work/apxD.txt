APPENDIX D – A Label-History
Benchmark for the Next-Category Task
This appendix answers one question. How much of the next category can be predicted
from the category history of the input window alone, without reading any representation at all?
The answer fixes how strong a screening argument in Chapter 5 can honestly be.
The screening procedure works as follows. For each candidate representation, a linear
model is trained to predict the next category from the vector of the last visit in the window
alone, and the macro-F1 it reaches is compared against the score a reference encoder reaches on
the same task. A candidate scoring far above that reference encoder is suspected of carrying the
label it is supposed to predict, and is discarded. The probe is run twice, once on standardized
features and once on raw ones, and either run can disqualify a candidate.
All scores in this appendix are macro-F1 on a zero-to-one scale, and differences between
them are quoted in points, where one point is 0.01 on that scale.
Two quantities are involved in that comparison. Both have served as the screen’s reference
level at different times, and both have been called the ceiling, which is the confusion this appendix
exists to remove. Each is given one name here and keeps it.
The label-history benchmark. The best macro-F1 obtained from the recorded category history
of the input window alone. No representation is read and no encoder is involved, so this is
a property of the sequence of category labels. It is what this appendix measures.
The clean reference encoder. The encoder that a screened candidate is actually compared
against, and the score the screening margin is applied to. Two encoders play this role
at Florida, both from the lineage the representation of Chapter 5 descends from: one at
0.4090 macro-F1 on standardized features and 0.4074 on raw ones, and a residual variant
at 0.4197 and 0.4182. Where the two are discussed together below, they are named in the
plural for that reason.
The benchmark is measured under four predictors that see category labels and nothing
else. The first is persistence, which repeats the last visited category and fits no parameters. The
second is a multinomial logistic model on a one-hot encoding of that same last category. The
third is the same model on the counts of each category over the nine visits of the window. The
fourth is the same model on positional one-hot encodings of all nine slots, and it is the only one of
the four that can use the order in which the categories occurred. The benchmark is the best score
of the four. All of them run under the protocol the screen itself uses: five-fold cross-validation

Two readings follow from the table, and they point in different directions.
The screening comparison is unaffected. Its verdicts are relative, since a candidate is
discarded when it clears the clean reference encoder by more than the screening margin of three
points, and the benchmark enters that arithmetic nowhere. The attention-based encoder that was
discarded at Florida clears the clean reference encoder by 8.9 points, so its verdict does not
depend on where the benchmark lies.
The absolute reading is the weaker one. At Florida the two clean reference encoders
score above the benchmark, 0.4090 and 0.4197 against 0.3617, which Chapter 5 reports as a gap
of four to six points, and so does every candidate screened beside them on the raw run of the
probe. One candidate falls below the benchmark on the standardized run, a relation-typed graph
encoder at 0.3328 that rises to 0.4142 on the raw one. It is not an example of a clean encoder
sitting below the benchmark, because it is the candidate that passed this screen and then leaked
under a downstream sequence model, which Chapter 5 reports as the reason the linear form is
a screen rather than a proof. What the screen bounds is therefore the candidates against each
other, not any of them against an absolute standard. That is the weaker of the two claims, and it
is the one Chapter 5 makes.

The gap is not by itself evidence of a leak. A per-visit vector legitimately carries more
than the previous category. It also carries the place, the neighborhood of that place in the graph,
and the hour of the visit, and any of those can predict the next category with no information
flowing backward in time.
The benchmark is also not an upper bound. It is the best score of four named predictors,
so a different model reading the same category history could score above it. Calling it a ceiling,
as the internal screening record does, would assert more than the measurement supports.
Two coverage limits apply. Texas is absent because the check-in graph the computation
reads was not retained for that dataset under the shipped representation. Only its embedding
outputs remain, and measuring the benchmark there would require re-running the preprocessing.
For Istanbul, 196 of the 29,816 places carry more than one category across the record. The reason
is not recorded in the data, so none is claimed here. The table maps each place to its most frequent
category. A strict variant that instead drops every window ending at one of those places keeps
55,946 of the 58,075 windows and gives 0.3009, against the 0.3016 of the table.
The computation is released with the code as <scripts/embedding_eval/autoc
orrelation_ceiling.py>. Its outputs are <autocorrelation_ceiling.json> and two
accompanying comma-separated files, all three under <docs/results/embedding_eval/re
screen_cat/>. Those file names keep the older word, since they predate the naming settled
here.
