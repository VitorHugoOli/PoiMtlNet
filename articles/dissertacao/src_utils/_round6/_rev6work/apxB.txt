B.4
Two extractions of the same Gowalla corpus
Chapters 3 and 4 report the Florida subset of Gowalla as 20,301 users, 65,009 POIs, and
990,518 check-ins, while Chapter 5 reports 21,052 users, 76,544 POIs, and 1,407,034 check-ins
for the same state of the same public dataset. The two are different extractions of that state, not
different datasets and not a discrepancy between them: the first two chapters report the corpus
as the extraction pipeline of their time produced it, and Chapter 5 reports the current extraction.
The mechanism is the category mapping, not the raw data and not a different filtering rule.
The extraction assigns a category to each check-in from a mapping table and discards the rows
whose category does not map, so widening that table keeps rows that an earlier run had dropped.
The mapping files were extended about eleven months after the earlier Florida extraction, and the
places they added fall mostly in Entertainment, Outdoors, and Travel. A controlled comparison
against the working extraction of the earlier period confirms the direction: each of its POIs,
users, and check-ins reappears in the current extraction, which adds further ones. The current
pipeline therefore cannot reproduce the earlier counts, and the two sets of figures are kept as
each chapter reported them rather than retrofitted to one another. The comparison is recorded in
the research repository, in src_utils/cbic_recompute_result.md.
B.5
The scope of the static task in Chapter 4
Chapter 4 reports two tasks. One is sequential: predict the category of the next place a
user visits, from the history of that user’s check-ins. The other is static: classify the category
of a place from that place’s own representation. This section records what we established after

===== PDF PAGE 99 =====
APPENDIX B. Errata to the Reproduced Articles
99
publication about the second one, and it bears on how that chapter’s category results should be
read.
The place embedding that Chapter 4 feeds to the static task is built from a fine-grained
class label attached to each place, and that label determines the target category exactly. Measured
over the five Gowalla state subsets the collection uses, the fine class takes between 284 and 365
distinct values per state, every one of them maps to exactly one of the seven top-level categories,
and none maps to more than one. The embedding is a lookup on the fine class, and the fine class
is a strict refinement of the label. So the static task’s input determines its target by construction,
and the reported accuracy on that task measures a deterministic mapping rather than a learned
semantic inference.
Three qualifications matter, and none of them is a softening.
The first is that the sequential task is unaffected. Its input is a history of visits and its target
is a category the model has not seen for that step, so no equivalent identity exists between input
and label. Every claim Chapter 4 makes about the sequential task, including the improvement
that the decomposed representation produces, stands as published.
The second is that this is specific to Chapter 4’s representation and does not carry to
Chapter 3. The earlier chapter builds a node feature from the average of a place’s neighbors’
one-hot categories, excluding the place itself, so a place’s own label never enters its own
representation. The two chapters use different embeddings, and the property described here
belongs to one of them.
The third is that Chapter 5 does not inherit the problem, for the reason that chapter gives
on its own terms: it replaces the place-level embedding with a check-in-level representation,
whose input is a visit rather than a place, and the identity described above does not arise.
We state this here rather than in the chapter because Chapter 4 is a published, co-authored
article and this appendix is the instrument this collection uses for the difference between what
the published text says and what we know now. The measurement is recorded in the repository
released with this work.
