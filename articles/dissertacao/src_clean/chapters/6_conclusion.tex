\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This dissertation asked whether multitask learning helps point-of-interest prediction
for the next category and the next region of a visit, and which design choices
determine the answer. Three
studies addressed this question. The first developed a joint model that did not
consistently outperform its dedicated counterparts (Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic}). The second
tested the input representation as one possible cause of that result and found that it
was the main bottleneck in that configuration (Chapter~\ref{ch:courb}). The third used a
check-in-level representation and a redesigned sharing topology
(Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac}). With this design, the joint model outperforms the dedicated
single-task models on the category task at all six datasets and on the region task at
four of the six. At the other two, it remains statistically non-inferior within a
two-point margin (TOST).

\section{Contributions by chapter}
\label{sec:conc:contributions}

Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic} presents MTLnet, the first joint model developed in this
research, and reports its negative result. The model combined static category
classification and next-category prediction. Its lower layers were shared between the
tasks over a place-level graph embedding. Even so, the joint model did not
consistently outperform the dedicated single-task models on either task, and it cost
more to train. The main contribution of the chapter is this finding and the analysis
behind it. The analysis identifies three possible causes: task dissimilarity and the
resulting risk of negative transfer, an input representation that may be insufficient
for both tasks, and an overly rigid sharing topology. This verdict applies to one
configuration, a place-level input under hard sharing. The following studies examine
how the result changes when these conditions change.

Chapter~\ref{ch:courb} kept the MTLnet architecture fixed and replaced the single
64-dimensional place embedding with decomposed spatial, temporal, and categorical
encoders. The chapter reports results for both tasks. On the static task, category
macro-F1 rose by 20.2 to 22.0 percentage points across the three states tested. This
increase was larger than the improvements obtained from the architectural variations
evaluated in the first study.

That gain needs two qualifications. The static task classifies a place from that
place's own representation, so its input already determines its target. The gain is not
evidence about the sequential task. The comparison is also not width-matched. The
decomposed input is wider than the place embedding that it replaces, 192 dimensions
against 64, and Chapter~\ref{ch:courb} calls for an equal-dimension control to separate
what the encoders add semantically from what the extra width adds on its own.

The sequential results provide the relevant diagnosis. For that task, the input does
not determine the target, and the decomposed encoders are more effective in most of
the cases tested. Because the sharing topology remained fixed, these results identify
the input representation as the main bottleneck in that configuration.

Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} builds on this diagnosis with Check2HGI, a check-in-level
representation that gives each visit its own vector instead of assigning the same
vector to every visit to a place. The joint model uses cross-attention to share
information between two task-specific
streams, while a private spatial path serves the region task. The evaluation used
user-disjoint cross-validation with twenty
fitted models per configuration, four seeds over one fixed set of five folds, and paired
tests on the four per-seed means. Under that protocol, the joint model outperforms the
dedicated single-task models on the category task at all six datasets, by 5.3 to 9.4
macro-F1 points. On the region task, it outperforms them at four datasets: Istanbul,
Florida, California, and Texas. At Alabama and Arizona, it remains statistically
non-inferior within a two-point margin (TOST). Non-inferiority was the registered
primary analysis for the region task. The four superiority findings are secondary
results outside that analysis plan.

\section{The consolidated answer}
\label{sec:conc:answer}

Does multitask learning help point-of-interest prediction? The answer is conditional.
Identifying those conditions is the dissertation's main finding. With a place-level
embedding and naive hard sharing, the joint model of Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic} did not
consistently outperform the two dedicated models. Negative transfer between the static
and sequential tasks was one possible explanation. With a check-in-level
representation and a sharing topology designed for it, one model produces both
predictions in a single forward pass. It outperforms the dedicated models on the
category task at all six datasets and on the region task at four of the six. At the
other two, it remains statistically non-inferior within a two-point margin (TOST).
The benefit therefore depends on the input representation and the sharing topology.

The first control asks whether the category improvement comes from the training signal
of the next-region task, whose output space is much larger. Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac}
tests this explanation by fixing the region pathway at its initial values. This removes
learning from the region side, so that task cannot provide a training signal to the
category task, but it leaves the rest of the joint architecture in place. The control
used one random initialization over five folds and was designed for one matched
comparison: the fixed-region joint model against the dedicated category model under
the same training configuration. At Alabama, Arizona, and Florida, the fixed-region
model retains its category advantage. Within this control, the category improvement
therefore does not require training transfer from the region task. The result rules out
that explanation, but it does not determine whether the gain comes from the category
encoder, the feed-forward blocks, the added depth, cross-attention, or a combination of
these components.

A separate Florida ablation examines cross-attention more directly. Removing the
cross-attention stack changed next-category macro-F1 by $-0.04 \pm 0.13$, a difference
that the paired Wilcoxon test could not distinguish from zero. This result is not
evidence that cross-attention has no effect on the final model. The ablation used a
configuration with a region-transition prior that is absent from the final models, and
the remaining components could adapt to the removal of the stack during training. They
may therefore have compensated for it. The available evidence supports attributing
the category gain to the joint architecture as a whole, not to one component.

The second control uses a capacity-matched dedicated baseline to test whether model
size alone explains the gain. At Alabama, the dedicated category model was widened
from about 0.6 million parameters to about 4.2 million, the joint model's budget. It
was fitted under three training configurations, with twenty models per configuration
and sixty in total. The best configuration reaches $56.16 \pm 1.89$ macro-F1, reported
as the mean and standard deviation over its twenty fitted models. The same model at its
own tuned width reaches $56.82 \pm 0.03$, while the joint model reaches
$64.51 \pm 0.09$. California shows the same pattern. At a hidden dimension of 752, the
widened model has 101.9 percent of the joint model's parameter count and reaches
$69.88 \pm 0.26$ macro-F1 over its twenty fitted models. The same model at its tuned
narrow width reaches $70.60 \pm 0.07$, while the joint model reaches
$77.05 \pm 0.01$.

At both datasets, widening leaves the dedicated model slightly below its narrow-width
optimum: 0.66 points below at Alabama and 0.72 at California. It recovers none of the
joint model's gain. The widened models were tuned separately. At both datasets, their
best training configuration used a lower learning rate than the best configuration of
the narrow model. However, the narrow models were tuned over a wider search than the
three configurations evaluated at Alabama and the two evaluated at California for the
widened models. Within this scope, increasing the parameter count alone did not improve
the dedicated model. Together, the controls exclude two explanations within the
settings in which they were evaluated: direct training transfer from the region task
and parameter count alone. They do not isolate which remaining component of the joint
architecture produces the gain.

The gradient measurements support the same interpretation within their limited scope.
For four Gowalla states, an auxiliary analysis on a separate data preparation obtained
an average cosine similarity of $+0.001$ between the task gradients over four seeds.
Three of those states are among the five reported in this dissertation. This result
measures directional conflict only and applies to this pair of tasks.
Appendix~\ref{apx:cosine} evaluates the same quantity for the final model over seven
datasets: the six reported here and one additional Gowalla state. It also finds cosine
values close to zero. Under the check-in-level representation, these results indicate
little directional conflict between the two sequential tasks. They are also consistent
with the small effect of gradient-balancing optimizers in this configuration and with
the effectiveness of a tuned fixed weighting.

The negative result of Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic} and the positive result of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} do not contradict each other. Together, they define the scope
of the claim. Multitask learning does not provide an automatic benefit, but the first
negative result does not rule it out. This dissertation demonstrates one path from the
negative result to a positive one and identifies the conditions tested along that path.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conc:limitations}

Six limitations bound the scope of these conclusions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Data vintage.} The five state datasets come from
        Gowalla~\cite{cho2011gowalla}, and the extraction used here spans January 2009
        to August 2011 across the five states. Mobility patterns, place inventories,
        and check-in behavior have changed since.
  \item \textbf{Taxonomy coarseness.} Next-category prediction uses seven top-level
        classes. A finer taxonomy may change the effect of joint training.
  \item \textbf{Transductive representation.} Check2HGI is trained on the check-in
        graph of each dataset, so it cannot represent unseen places or users without
        retraining.
  \item \textbf{No next-place task.} The experiments do not predict the exact next
        POI, and their conclusions apply only to next category and next region.
  \item \textbf{Geographic coverage.} Outside the United States, the evidence rests on
        a single city, Istanbul.
  \item \textbf{The task-pair confound.} The task pair changed together with the
        representation and the sharing topology. Chapters~\ref{ch:cbic}
        and~\ref{ch:courb} paired static category classification with next-category
        prediction, while Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} pairs two sequential targets,
        next-category and next-region prediction. No single controlled ablation
        separates the representation-and-topology change from the task-pair change in
        the final result. Chapter~\ref{ch:courb} is the fixed-pair control for the
        diagnosis. The capacity-matched baseline above addresses the parameter-count
        explanation only in the setting that it tested: the category task, two of the
        six datasets, one width point per dataset, and width scaling rather than
        depth. The greater similarity of the final pair may still contribute to the
        size of the improvement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:conc:future}

Future work follows directly from these limitations. Newer and denser traces would
test the conclusions beyond the Gowalla vintage (limitation~1), and finer-grained
taxonomies would test them beyond the seven-class division (limitation~2). Because the
check-in-level representation is trained on a fixed graph, an inductive variant of it,
one that embeds unseen places and users without retraining, would remove the
transductive constraint and support deployment in growing cities (limitation~3).

Adding the exact next place as a third target would extend the joint model beyond the
two properties predicted in this work. The cascade architectures reviewed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} suggest that category and region predictions can provide
additional structure for this task (limitation~4).
Further cities outside the United States would widen the geographic base
(limitation~5). Training the Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} joint model on the
Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic} task pair, under the check-in-level representation, is the
controlled comparison needed to isolate the effect of changing the task pair
(limitation~6).

\section{Final remarks}
\label{sec:conc:final}

The practical output of this dissertation is a single model that predicts two
properties of the next visit in one forward pass: what kind of place the user will
visit and in which part of the city. The methodological contribution is the sequence
of evidence that led to this model: a published negative result, the diagnosis that
identified the input representation as the main bottleneck, and a solution designed
from that diagnosis. The negative result was not an obstacle to the contribution. It
was its first half.
