\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This dissertation asked whether multitask learning helps point-of-interest prediction
for the next category and the next region, and what the answer depends on. Three
studies answered it. The first built a joint model that did not consistently
outperform its dedicated counterparts (Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic}). Because that result
left several possible causes open, the second study tested one of them and located the
bottleneck in the input representation (Chapter~\ref{ch:courb}). The third then built
a check-in-level representation with a redesigned sharing topology, and under it one
joint model outperforms the dedicated single-task models on the category task at all
six datasets and on the region task at four of the six, and is statistically
non-inferior to them within a two-point margin (TOST) at the other two
(Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac}).

\section{Contributions by chapter}
\label{sec:conc:contributions}

MTLnet was the first joint model of this research, chapter~\ref{ch:cbic} presents it,
and an honest negative result with it. The model trained two tasks at once, static
category classification and next-category prediction, and shared its lower layers
between them over a place-level graph embedding. Both were standard choices at the
time. Even so, the joint model did not consistently outperform the dedicated
single-task models on either task, and it cost more to train. The chapter matters for
that finding, and for the analysis behind it, more than for the architecture. It names
three possible causes: task dissimilarity and the negative transfer it can bring, an
input representation too poor to serve both tasks at once, and hard sharing that was
too rigid. The rest of the dissertation tested all three. That verdict held for one
configuration, a place-level input under hard sharing, so the later chapters were free
to revise it.


Chapter~\ref{ch:courb} kept the MTLnet architecture as it was and replaced the single
64-dimensional place embedding with decomposed spatial, temporal, and categorical
encoders. It reports both tasks. On the static task, category macro-F1 rose by 20.2 to
22.0 percentage points across the three states tested, a larger gain than any
architectural variation had produced before it.

That gain needs two qualifications. The static task classifies a place from that
place's own representation, so its input already determines its target. The gain is not
evidence about the sequential task. The comparison is also not width-matched. The
decomposed input is wider than the place embedding that it replaces, 192 dimensions
against 64, and Chapter~\ref{ch:courb} calls for an equal-dimension control to separate
what the encoders add semantically from what the extra width adds on its own.

The sequential results of that chapter carry the diagnosis instead. There the input
does not determine the target, and the decomposed encoders are more effective in most
of the cases tested. The input representation, not the sharing topology, was the
bottleneck at that stage, and Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} measures the direction and the
size of that effect.

The resolution arrived in Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac}, and it begins with the
representation. Check2HGI gives each visit its own vector, instead of assigning the same
vector to every visit to a place. The joint model built on it shares through a
cross-attention trunk between two task-specific streams, and it keeps a private spatial
path for the region task. The evaluation used user-disjoint cross-validation with twenty
fitted models per configuration, four seeds over one fixed set of five folds, and paired
tests on the four per-seed means. Under that protocol, the joint model outperforms the
dedicated single-task models on the category task at all six datasets, by 5.3 to 9.4
macro-F1 points. On the region task it outperforms them at four of six, Istanbul,
Florida, California, and Texas, and at Alabama and Arizona it remains statistically
non-inferior within a two-point margin (TOST). The registered primary analysis for the
region task was non-inferiority. The four region-superiority findings are secondary
results outside that plan and are reported as such rather than reclassified after the
analysis.

\section{The consolidated answer}
\label{sec:conc:answer}

Does multitask learning help point-of-interest prediction? The answer is conditional, and identifying the
condition is the dissertation's main finding. With a place-level embedding and naive hard sharing,
no: the joint model of Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic} did not consistently outperform two
dedicated models, and negative transfer between the static and the sequential task was
among the causes it considered. With a check-in-level representation and a sharing
topology built for it, yes: one model produces both predictions in one forward pass,
and it outperforms the dedicated models on the category task at all six datasets and on
the region task at four of the six, while remaining statistically non-inferior to them
within a two-point margin (TOST) at the other two. The answer therefore depends on the
representation and on the sharing topology built for it.

Two controls separate this claim from wishful attribution. The first, reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac}, fixes the region pathway at its initial values, so that it can
neither learn nor teach the category task. Yet the category gain survives at the three
datasets where the control was run, Alabama, Arizona, and Florida, so the gain does not
come from the region task teaching the category task; it comes from something in the
joint architecture that the dedicated model does not have. The control does not say
which part. One
development ablation at Florida removed the cross-attention stack alone and moved
next-category macro-F1 by $-0.04 \pm 0.13$, but that measurement was taken on an
earlier configuration whose region output was driven by a region-transition prior that
the models reported here do not use. Its own record reads the null as a compensation
effect, in which the remaining components absorb the removed stack's role, rather than
as an absence of contribution. For that reason, this dissertation does not name the shared trunk as the source, and it does not offer the ablation as evidence that the trunk
contributes nothing.

The second control is a capacity-matched dedicated baseline, reported here as a
frame-level analysis because it was run after the Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} manuscript
was submitted. It asks whether size alone explains the gain. At Alabama, the dedicated
category model was widened from about 0.6 million parameters to about 4.2 million, the
joint model's budget, and fitted under three training configurations, twenty models
each and sixty in total. Its best configuration averages $56.16 \pm 1.89$ macro-F1,
mean and standard deviation over its twenty fitted models, while the same model at its
own tuned width reaches $56.82 \pm 0.03$ and the joint model reaches $64.51 \pm 0.09$.
California repeats the pattern at a hidden dimension of 752, which is 101.9 percent of
the joint model's parameter count: there the widened model reaches $69.88 \pm 0.26$
macro-F1 over its twenty fitted models, against $70.60 \pm 0.07$ for the same model at
its tuned narrow width and $77.05 \pm 0.01$ for the joint model.

At both datasets, then, widening leaves the dedicated model at or slightly below its
narrow-width optimum, 0.66 points below at Alabama and 0.72 at California. It recovers
none of the joint model's gain. One detail supports the fairness of the comparison: at
both datasets the widened model's best training configuration uses a lower learning
rate than the narrow width's, so the sweep found the wide model's own better setting
rather than rerunning the narrow model's. The narrow width's tuned setting, though,
came from a wider sweep of training configurations than the three at Alabama and the
two at California tried here. Parameter count alone, without the second task's training
signal, therefore yields nothing in this setting. Together, the two controls support
what Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} already states as its finding: the gain is not cross-task
transfer.

At the gradient level, the picture is consistent with this reading, within its
measurement scope. During development, on an earlier preparation of the data, the
cosine similarity between the two tasks' gradients averaged $+0.001$ over four seeds on
four Gowalla states, three of which are among the five states reported here. That
figure concerns directional conflict only, and it is a finding for this pair of tasks
rather than a general rule. Appendix~\ref{apx:cosine} reports the same quantity on the
final model over seven datasets, the six reported here and one further Gowalla state,
and reaches the same conclusion from the larger measurement. Under the check-in-level representation, the two sequential tasks coexist with gradients close to orthogonal, so
sharing stopped hurting. That also explains why gradient-balancing optimizers had
little to correct in this configuration, and why a tuned fixed weighting sufficed.

Taken together, the negative result of Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic} and the positive result of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} do not contradict each other; they bound the claim. Multitask
learning is not an automatic gain that any architecture obtains, and it is not ruled
out by the first negative result. This dissertation adds one worked path from the
negative result to the positive one, with the conditions named and tested.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conc:limitations}

Six limitations bound the scope of these conclusions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Data vintage.} The five state datasets come from
        Gowalla~\cite{cho2011gowalla}, and the extraction used here spans January 2009
        to August 2011 across the five states. Mobility patterns, place inventories,
        and check-in behavior have changed since.
  \item \textbf{Taxonomy coarseness.} Next-category prediction uses seven top-level
        classes. A finer taxonomy may change the effect of joint training.
  \item \textbf{Transductive representation.} Check2HGI is trained on the check-in
        graph of each dataset, so it cannot represent unseen places or users without
        retraining.
  \item \textbf{No next-place task.} The experiments do not predict the exact next
        POI, and their conclusions apply only to next category and next region.
  \item \textbf{Geographic coverage.} Outside the United States, the evidence rests on
        a single city, Istanbul.
  \item \textbf{The task-pair confound.} The task pair changed together with the
        representation and the sharing topology. Chapters~\ref{ch:cbic}
        and~\ref{ch:courb} paired static category classification with next-category
        prediction, while Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} pairs two sequential targets,
        next-category and next-region prediction. No single controlled ablation
        separates the representation-and-topology change from the task-pair change in
        the final result. Chapter~\ref{ch:courb} is the fixed-pair control for the
        diagnosis, and the capacity-matched baseline above closes the parameter-count
        explanation only in the setting that it tested: the category task, two of the
        six datasets, one width point per dataset, and width scaling rather than
        depth. The greater similarity of the final pair may still contribute to the
        size of the improvement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:conc:future}

Each limitation names its own next step. Newer and denser traces would test the
conclusions beyond the Gowalla vintage (limitation~1), and finer-grained taxonomies
would test them beyond the seven-class division (limitation~2). Because the
check-in-level representation is trained on a fixed graph, an inductive variant of it,
one that embeds unseen places and users without retraining, would remove the
transductive constraint and open deployment to growing cities (limitation~3).

Adding the exact next place as a third target, on top of the two properties this work
predicts, would reconnect the joint model to the field's hardest task, where the
cascade architectures reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} suggest that category and
region predictions can serve as structure rather than competition (limitation~4).
Further cities outside the United States would widen the geographic base
(limitation~5). And training the Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} joint model on the
Chapter~\ref{ch:cbic} task pair, under the check-in-level representation, is the
fixed-pair ablation that would close the task-pair confound (limitation~6).

\section{Final remarks}
\label{sec:conc:final}

The practical output of this dissertation is one deployable model whose single forward
pass answers two questions that a mobility-aware service asks about the next visit,
what kind of place and in which part of the city. Its methodological output is an
honest record: a published negative result, the diagnosis that turned it into a
question about representations, and the resolution that answered that question. The
negative result was not an obstacle on the way to the contribution. It was the
contribution's first half.
