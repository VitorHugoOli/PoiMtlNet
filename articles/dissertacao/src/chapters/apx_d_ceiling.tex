
\chapter{The label-only ceiling for the next-category task}
\label{apx:ceiling}

Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} screens each candidate representation with a per-visit probe: it predicts
the next category from the last window slot alone. A vector that carries only the past cannot beat
what the past itself allows, so the screen needs a reference level. The screening record uses a
clean reference encoder for that role and, in places, describes that encoder's score as the
autocorrelation ceiling. The two are not the same quantity, and this appendix separates them.

The ceiling is a property of the label sequence, not of any encoder. It is the best macro-F1
obtainable when the only information available is the genuine category history of the input window.
No embedding is read. Table~\ref{tab:apx:ceiling} reports it under four label-only predictors: the
persistence rule, which repeats the last visited category; a balanced multinomial logistic model on
a one-hot of that category; the same model on category counts over the nine-visit window; and the
same model on positional one-hots of all nine slots. The ceiling is the best of the four, since a
ceiling is the most that the restricted information permits. The protocol is the one the screen
itself uses: five folds grouped by user, macro-F1, averaged over folds. Class weights are balanced
because the reported metric is macro-F1, which weights the rare categories equally.

Two readings follow, and they point in different directions.

The screen's own comparison is unaffected. Its verdicts are relative: an encoder is disqualified
when it clears the clean reference by more than the screen's margin of three points. The
attention-based encoder that was disqualified at Florida clears that reference by 8.9 points, so
the decision does not depend on where the label-only ceiling sits.

The absolute reading is weaker than the screening record suggests. Every encoder screened at
Florida, the clean references included, scores above the label-only ceiling: 0.4090 and 0.4197
against 0.3617, a gap of four to six points. That gap is not by itself evidence of a leak. A
per-visit vector legitimately carries more than the previous category, including the place itself,
its graph neighborhood, and the hour of the visit, and any of those can predict the next category
without any information flowing backward in time. What it does mean is that the screen bounds
encoders against each other rather than against an absolute standard, which is the weaker of the
two claims and the one Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} now makes.

Two coverage limits apply. Texas is absent because the artifacts the computation needs, the
check-in graph and the window file, were not retained for that dataset under the shipped
representation; only its embedding outputs remain, and recomputing the ceiling there would require
re-running preprocessing. For Istanbul, 196 of 29,816 places carry more than one category across
the record, since venues were re-categorized over time; the table uses each place's modal category,
and dropping every ambiguous place instead moves the Istanbul ceiling by less than a thousandth
(0.3009 against 0.3016).

The computation is released with the code, as
\path{scripts/embedding_eval/autocorrelation_ceiling.py}; its outputs are the file
\path{autocorrelation_ceiling.json} and two accompanying comma-separated files, all three under
\path{docs/results/embedding_eval/rescreen_cat/}.
