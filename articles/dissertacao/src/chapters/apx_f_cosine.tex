\chapter{Why the Two Tasks Do Not Compete on the Shared Trunk}
\label{apx:cosine}

A joint model that shares parameters between two tasks can be worse at both than two dedicated
models are at one each. The usual reason is that the tasks disagree about how the shared
parameters should change, and the disagreement is visible in the gradients: if the update that
next-region asks for points against the update that next-category asks for, one task improves
at the other's expense. This appendix measures whether that disagreement occurs in the joint
model of Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac}. It does not, and the measurement says something stronger
than the absence of conflict.

\section{What was measured, and on what unit}
\label{apx:cosine:setup}

At every training epoch, the two task losses were each back-propagated to the shared
cross-attention trunk, and the cosine of the angle between the two resulting gradient vectors
was recorded. The cosine is the natural quantity here because it is scale-free: it reports the
alignment of the two requested updates and ignores their magnitudes, which differ between the
tasks and change during training. A cosine of $+1$ means the two tasks ask for the same update,
$-1$ that they ask for opposite updates, and $0$ that the two requests are orthogonal, meaning
each leaves the other's objective unchanged to first order.

The observations are 3,900 epoch-level cosines from four datasets. Florida contributes 3,150,
spanning twelve configurations that vary the loss weight, the schedule of the weighting
coefficient, and the training procedure. Alabama, Arizona, and Georgia contribute 250 each, at
the canonical configuration. Every run is five-fold user-disjoint cross-validation over fifty
epochs, so one configuration on one dataset is five series of fifty values.

Three of those four datasets, Florida, Alabama, and Arizona, are among the six on which
Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} reports its results. The fourth, Georgia, is a further Gowalla state
that the dissertation does not otherwise use; it enters here because this diagnostic could be run
on it cheaply, and it is reported because leaving out a measured dataset would be a choice about
the evidence. Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} states the same conclusion from a smaller measurement made
during development, on an earlier preparation of the data and over four seeds rather than the
per-epoch series used here. The two are separate measurements of the same quantity, and their
numbers are not interchangeable: this appendix supersedes nothing in that chapter and repeats none
of its figures.

training run, and a statistic that treats them as independent draws will report more confidence
than the data hold. The unit of independence is the fold, and for Florida, whose twelve
configurations reuse the same five folds, the configuration. Every test reported below is
therefore computed on fold means for Alabama, Arizona, and Georgia, five per dataset, and on
configuration means for Florida, twelve in all. Where a count of the underlying observations
appears, it describes the data and not the sample size of a test.

The margin is a judgment about relevance and is stated as one: at
$|\cos| < 0.05$ the projection of one task's update onto the other's direction is under five
percent of its length, which is smaller than the epoch-to-epoch variation in either gradient
and smaller than any effect a gradient-balancing method could act on. The two-point margin used
for the region task in Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} is a different quantity on a different scale,
and the two should not be read against each other.

\section{The result}
\label{apx:cosine:result}

Every dataset is equivalent to zero, and Table~\ref{tab:apx:cosine} gives each one's mean, its
confidence interval and its tests. All four means lie within one and a half hundredths of zero,
against a margin of five hundredths. Equivalence also holds at every other level of aggregation,
including the raw observations, so the conclusion does not depend on the choice of unit.

The distinction between this and a null result is the point of the appendix. A test that failed
to reject zero would license only the statement that no conflict was detected, which is
compatible with a conflict too small to see at this sample size. An equivalence test licenses
the positive statement: the mean alignment lies inside a margin fixed in advance, so whatever
alignment exists is too small to matter. That is a claim about the tasks, not about the power of
the experiment.

Figure~\ref{fig:apx:cosine} shows the distributions. One feature of it needs saying plainly.
The equivalence is a statement about the mean, not about every observation: 91.3 percent of the
3,900 cosines fall inside the margin, and the full range runs from $-0.34$ to $+0.58$. Single
epochs do drift out of the margin in both directions, which is ordinary noise in a quantity
computed from one mini-batch. What does not happen is a systematic pull to either side.

Two departures from a flat, uniform picture are worth reporting rather than smoothing, and both
appear on the smaller datasets.

The first is a small positive tendency that recurs. On Alabama all five fold means are positive,
averaging $+0.0112$, and on Georgia all five are positive as well, averaging $+0.0039$; both
have confidence intervals that exclude zero, and both are equivalent to zero under the margin.
Two datasets showing the same direction independently is a more interesting observation than
either alone would be. It is still not a significant effect, and the reason is worth stating
because it is a property of the design rather than of the data: the exact sign test returns
$0.0625$ for both, which is the smallest value it can return at five observations, so no
distribution-free test could reach significance here however consistent the pattern. A
$t$-test does reject at both ($p = 0.013$ and $p = 0.009$), but at five observations that
rests entirely on assuming normality. The defensible reading is a consistent positive tendency
that recurs across datasets and that five folds per dataset cannot establish. Its direction is
worth one further remark: a positive cosine is mild cooperation between the tasks, not conflict,
so what the smaller datasets hint at points away from interference rather than toward it.

The second is a decline over training. On Alabama the mean cosine falls from about $+0.058$ over
the first five epochs toward zero, and the fall survives a fold-level test on the per-fold slopes
($p = 0.006$, all five slopes negative); Georgia shows the same in weaker form ($p = 0.047$,
also all five negative), while Florida and Arizona are flat ($p = 0.71$ and $p = 0.57$). Both
declines rest on five folds, against the sixty series behind Florida's estimate, so they are the
least precisely estimated trends in the set. The decline stays inside the margin throughout, it
moves the cosine toward zero rather than away from it, and no mechanism is proposed for it.

\section{What orthogonality explains}
\label{apx:cosine:mechanism}

Orthogonal gradients mean the two tasks' updates neither reinforce nor cancel on the shared
parameters. Each task's requested update has almost no component along the other's direction,
so the trunk can serve both without either task's improvement being paid for by the other. Two
consequences follow, and they are the reason this measurement belongs in the dissertation
rather than in a laboratory notebook.

The first concerns gradient-balancing methods. Nash-MTL, and the family of loss and gradient
balancers around it, exist to resolve exactly the disagreement measured here: they reweight or
reproject task gradients so that a dominant task cannot overwrite a weaker one. When the
gradients are already orthogonal there is nothing for such a method to resolve, which is why
hard sharing is not costly in this architecture and why Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} reports that no
balancer improved on a fixed loss weighting. The finding and the measurement agree, and the
measurement explains the finding.

The second concerns the arc of the three studies. The investigation began with the hypothesis
that the sharing scheme was what limited joint training, and the first study's null result was
read that way at the time. If the two tasks had been in conflict on the shared parameters, a
better sharing scheme or a better balancer would have been the remedy. They were not in
conflict, so the ceiling was elsewhere, and the second and third studies located it in the input
representation. This appendix is the mechanism behind the claim that representation mattered
more than the sharing scheme: there was little task interference to fix, and so little for a
change of architecture to recover.

\section{How far this extends, and how far it does not}
\label{apx:cosine:extension}

Four datasets and twelve configurations bound this result along two different axes, and the two
answer different objections.

The twelve Florida configurations vary the loss weight, the schedule of the weighting
coefficient, and the training procedure. Every one of them is equivalent to zero on its own five folds, and their
twelve means span $[-0.00261, +0.00457]$. So the orthogonality is not an artifact of a
particular choice of hyperparameters: it survives every hyperparameter and procedure that was
varied.

The four datasets vary the data instead. Among the three that Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} also
reports, the check-in counts run from 113,846 at Alabama to 1,407,034 at Florida and the region
counts from 1,109 to 4,703, so the axis spans an order of magnitude in volume; Georgia adds a
fourth state measured under the same protocol. So the result is also not a quirk of Florida.

What the two axes together suggest is that orthogonality is a property of this task pair rather
than of the tuning. Next category asks which of seven kinds of place a user visits next; next
region asks which tract or neighborhood they visit next. The two read the same trace and predict
different projections of the same event, and those projections appear to be close to
statistically independent given the shared representation, which would explain why the requested
updates carry so little of each other.

That is where the evidence stops, and the boundary matters. Every run measured here uses one
architecture family, the cross-attention joint model of Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac}. The
architecture is what determines where the two tasks meet and how much of the network they share,
so it is precisely the factor most likely to change the answer, and it is the one factor this
appendix did not vary. Nothing here says that the gradients would remain orthogonal in a model
that shares more of its depth, that couples the tasks in a cascade instead of side by side, or
that shares an output layer. A reader who wants to know should run the same diagnostic without
other changes: record the per-epoch cosine on the shared parameters of the candidate
architecture, under a fixed loss weighting, across the folds of one dataset, and test the fold
means for equivalence against a margin chosen before looking. If the cosine there is also
equivalent to zero, the property belongs to the task pair; if it is not, it belongs to this
architecture, and the reasoning in Section~\ref{apx:cosine:mechanism} applies only to models
shaped like this one.

Three of the six datasets of Chapter~\ref{ch:mobiwac} are not represented above. California,
Texas, and Istanbul are absent for reasons of computational cost rather than any difficulty of
principle: they are the largest of the six, and the region task at that scale forces full
single-precision training. The same diagnostic on those three needs no new method, only the
compute, and it is the obvious next measurement. Until it exists, this appendix covers three of
the dissertation's six datasets plus Georgia, and a reader should not extrapolate to the three
that are missing.
