\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The check-in-level representation improves the category task}
\label{sec:results-part1}
The check-in-level representation outperforms a standard place embedding
(HGI~\cite{huang2023hgi}) on next-category macro-F1 at every dataset
(Table~\ref{tab:substrate}). The comparison is controlled: target, single-task model, training configuration, windowing, and folds are identical; only the input representation changes (a vector per visit versus a vector per place), so the difference isolates the representation. Both arms also share the epoch budget, the precision, and the logit adjustment, so the only free variable is the input. The gap ranges from $+0.23$ (Florida) to $+6.29$ (Istanbul). It is modest in size and unusually consistent in sign: all five folds favor the check-in-level representation at every one of the six datasets, and the difference is significant under a paired test everywhere except Florida ($p=0.07$).
The advantage is a property of the per-visit vectors themselves rather than of the larger number of training vectors per place: the geometry below separates categories at the visit level and not at the place level, which is the distinction the task turns on.

The geometry of the vectors shows why the representation helps this task in
particular: the per-visit vectors' silhouette score by category (how tight and well separated the seven labeled groups are, on a $-1$ to $1$ scale) is about $0.53$ against about $0.00$ for the place embedding, and their nearest-neighbor category purity (the share of nearest neighbors with the vector's own category) is about $0.98$ against about $0.79$ (both averaged over Alabama, Arizona, and Florida). The same geometry does not separate regions, so the benefit is category-only (the joint model's spatial stream instead reads the region-level vectors of the same graph, Section~\ref{sec:method-model}). CTLE~\cite{lin2021ctle}, the closest prior contextual embedding, also gives each visit its own vector, but it pretrains on place identifiers and timestamps alone, so the category vocabulary never enters its training, whereas our graph reads each visit's category and time of day as input features. At Florida, we fine-tune CTLE together with the task model, using its authors' defaults and the same 64 dimensions and windowing as ours. It reaches $33.45$ macro-F1 at its best epoch and $29.69$ at its final one (an epoch is one pass over the training data), below both the place embedding under the same best-epoch rule and the check-in-level representation. With frozen weights, fed to the same single-task model, CTLE repeats the ordering at Alabama, Arizona, and Istanbul. A feature-concatenation control joins the place embedding with the same raw
per-visit features that our graph reads (the category one-hot plus hour and
day-of-week encodings) and feeds the result to the same single-task model. On the earlier
configuration of this study it raised the place embedding by $+2.0$, $+1.7$, and $+0.8$ macro-F1 at
Alabama, Arizona, and Florida. Read against the category gaps reported here, those margins are of the
same size as the gaps themselves or larger, so on the category axis this control does not separate
the hierarchical representation from the per-visit features it reads, and we do not claim that it
does. At Florida, where the control was also run on the region task, it moved the place embedding by
$-0.23$ Acc@10, so the injected features do not substitute for the graph on that axis, and the same
graph supplies the region-level vectors that the joint model's spatial stream consumes. We therefore
report the category advantage as a measured difference rather than as a decomposition of its causes.

\subsection{One model, two tasks}
\label{sec:results-part2}
One model serves both tasks without giving up either. On next region it
outperforms the dedicated model at Texas and California, the two datasets with the most regions,
and is statistically non-inferior within a two-point margin at the other four. On next category it
outperforms the dedicated model at Florida and is within a fifth of a point of it everywhere else.

For scale: always predicting the most common category reaches between $5.7$
and $7.3$ macro-F1 depending on the dataset (the majority-class floor, lowest at Florida, whose
category mix is the most even), and a random region top-ten guess
is right at most about two percent of the time (Section~\ref{sec:setup-metrics}).

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the single joint model against the dedicated
single-task ceilings (the level a joint model is usually expected, at
best, to match). Batch size and learning rate were searched for the dedicated category model at every dataset and
for the joint model at the smaller datasets; Texas and California carry the joint configuration
transferred from those searches rather than one validated on them, and the dedicated region models
use one fixed configuration throughout.
Throughout, every reported model is one saved artifact per fold, read at its
validation-selected epoch: each dedicated model at its task's best epoch, and
the joint model at the epoch selected by its joint validation score (the
geometric mean of the two task metrics), with both tasks read from that one
model. Reading each joint task at its own best epoch instead would raise every joint result, by at most
$0.17$ (category) and $0.90$ (region) points, but no single saved model attains both of those
numbers at once, which is why the served checkpoint is what we report.

Figure~\ref{fig:deltas} plots the signed differences ordered by region count. On
category, the joint model outperforms the dedicated model at Florida
($+0.19$ macro-F1) and lands within a fifth of a point of it at the other five datasets, above at
Istanbul and below at Alabama, Arizona, Texas, and California.
On region (Acc@10), the joint model outperforms the dedicated model at Texas
($+1.21$) and California ($+1.06$), the two datasets with the most regions, and stays a
non-inferior match (TOST, $\pm2$~pp) at Istanbul, Alabama, Arizona, and Florida. The two datasets
where sharing pays are the two largest region vocabularies, which is consistent with the reading
that a shared representation helps most where the region task is hardest; the ordering within that
pair does not follow region count, and region count and corpus size co-vary here, so we read the
grouping rather than a monotone law.

The statistical picture, seed by seed. On region, Texas and California are the two datasets where
all four seeds favor the joint model and the gain survives a Holm correction across the six datasets
(paired $t$ on the four per-seed means, Holm-corrected $p=0.00013$ at Texas and $p=0.0000063$ at California); their 90\% confidence intervals are $+1.13$ to $+1.29$ at
Texas and $+1.03$ to $+1.08$ at California. The remaining four are tested for equivalence (TOST,
$\pm2$~pp) and pass with intervals well inside the margin: Istanbul ($-0.08$; $-0.16$ to $-0.00$),
Alabama ($-0.87$; $-1.00$ to $-0.75$), Arizona ($-0.44$; $-0.62$ to $-0.25$), and Florida ($-0.16$;
$-0.19$ to $-0.13$). All four intervals lie below zero, and three of the four deficits survive a
Holm-corrected test in that direction (Alabama, Arizona, and Florida; Istanbul does not); every one
of them is far inside two points, so we report them as matches within the margin, not as gains. On category, Florida is the one dataset where all four seeds favor the joint model and the
gain clears the Holm correction (corrected $p=0.011$; $+0.14$ to $+0.25$). The other five category
differences are not resolved in either direction by a superiority test. We leave them at that: the
equivalence margin of Section~\ref{sec:setup-metrics} is registered for the region axis, and we do
not transfer it to category to convert an unresolved difference into a demonstrated match.

A reader used to multi-task learning~\cite{caruana1997multitask} expects the
harder task, here next-region with its hundreds to several thousand classes, to teach the easier one; a control shows otherwise. We freeze the region pathway at the start of training so it can neither learn nor teach the category
task. On the earlier configuration of this model, at Alabama, Arizona, and Florida, the category
result was unaffected by that intervention, which is a negative result worth reporting: the category
behavior of the joint model does not arise from the region task teaching the category one. That
control has not been repeated at the configuration reported here, so we state it as evidence about
the architecture rather than as a measurement of the present cells. The control fixes region training rather than any part of the category pathway, so it does not
identify which part of the joint architecture produces the region result, and we do not name the
shared trunk as its source. Direct ablations of the trunk, at the datasets where they were run,
move both tasks by less than two tenths of a point in either direction, which is below what a
single-seed five-fold comparison resolves. The decomposition is therefore open, and the trunk is
reported as one component of an architecture whose region result is measured, not as the component
that produces it. What the result does establish is operational: both answers come from one model
and one forward pass.

The joint model is also above every external baseline reported, on both tasks,
across all six datasets (Table~\ref{tab:results}): on region, the primary
region-native comparison HMT-GRN~\cite{lim2022hmtgrn}, STAN~\cite{luo2021stan}
trained from the raw check-ins, and a ReHDM reference~\cite{li2025rehdm}; on
category, POI-RGNN~\cite{capanema2023poirgnn} and the Markov-K floor. These externals run on their own embeddings, so this comparison
also includes the representation advantage of Section~\ref{sec:results-part1}; the
controlled comparison for the joint model remains the Dedicated column of
Table~\ref{tab:results}, which uses the same representation, windows, and
folds. The first-order Markov region floor of Section~\ref{sec:setup-baselines},
computed under our windows and folds, reaches $51$ to $72$ Acc@10 across the
datasets; the joint model exceeds it by $4.1$ to $10.0$ points on all six
datasets.

We prefer coupling the tasks in parallel rather than in a chain: neither target is
subordinate to the other, so a chain must pick one to predict first and pass its errors to the other. The choice still had to be checked, because CSLSL reports its chain outperforming a shared-trunk parallel variant on its own benchmarks~\cite{huang2024cslsl}. Rewired as a cascade
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-baselines}), our model reaches the same combined
score, the geometric mean of the two task metrics, as its parallel form: at Alabama, Arizona, Florida, and Istanbul, with both forms trained under the same configuration and initialization, the difference is about zero and neither task moves by more than a quarter of a point. We read this as a defense of the
parallel design, not a claim that we outperform the cascade: on this
representation the coupling topology makes no measurable difference, and the simpler parallel structure loses nothing. Two qualifications bound this
reading: the cascade runs under the configuration tuned for the parallel model, and
its form was fixed in advance, so tuning the cascade itself remains future work.

\subsection{External validity (Istanbul)}
\label{sec:results-external}
The joint-model results hold for a non-U.S. city under the same representation, sliding-window protocol, and training setup as the U.S. states. At Istanbul, the
joint model is within a tenth of a point of the dedicated model on both tasks: $+0.08$ macro-F1 on
category (dedicated $35.34$, joint $35.42$) and $-0.08$ Acc@10 on region (dedicated $75.16$, joint
$75.08$), the latter non-inferior within the two-point margin. The comparable quantity is the
difference against the dedicated model, not the absolute
Acc@10, since region counts differ across datasets (520 mahalle here). The external baselines show the same ordering on both tasks
(Table~\ref{tab:results}).
The pattern seen in the U.S. states, one model holding both tasks within the two-point margin of two
dedicated models, repeats on a different continent and a different region unit.
