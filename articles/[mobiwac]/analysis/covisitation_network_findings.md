# Co-visitation network structure of our check-in data

> Generated by `analysis/covisitation_network.py` on 2026-07-07. Replicates, as closely as feasible, the
> co-visitation-graph methodology of Moura, Aquino & Loureiro, "On the Design of Mobility-Aware Systems: A
> Tourist's Perspective," MSWiM 2025 (DOI 10.1109/MSWiM67937.2025.11308734) ("the Rome paper") on our own
> five Gowalla US states (Alabama, Arizona, Florida, California, Texas) plus Istanbul (Massive-STEPS). Raw
> numbers: `covisitation_network_results.json`. This file is the source for any paper sentence that leans on
> this experiment.

## Method (see the script docstring for full detail)

Nodes = distinct `placeid`. Undirected edge between two POIs whenever the same `userid` checked in at both
(binary "ever visited," not weighted by visit count); edge weight = number of distinct shared users. Built as
`P = M^T @ M` on a sparse binary user-by-place incidence matrix `M`, diagonal zeroed -- no explicit user-pair
loop. Global clustering coefficient (transitivity) is reported via an **unbiased wedge-sampling estimator**
(20,000 samples per graph), not `networkx.transitivity`: we tried the exact computation on Alabama, the
*smallest* dataset in this study (11,848 nodes, 4.7M edges), as a validation ground truth, and **killed it after
7+ minutes with no result** -- exact global transitivity is not tractable here at any scale we touch, not just
the large ones. Diameter / average shortest path is sampled (BFS from a random subset of giant-component nodes,
scipy Dijkstra-based `shortest_path(unweighted=True)` for graphs under ~400M edges; a hand-rolled O(V+E)
frontier BFS with a **very** small sample for the one graph above that -- California, see below). Giant-component
fraction and connected-component count are exact (`scipy.sparse.csgraph.connected_components`, linear in edge
count regardless of density). All figures below are single-run outputs (seed 0, no repeated trials beyond the
sampling estimators' own internal repetition).

Rome paper reference point: C = 0.533, density = 0.145 (C/density = 3.7x), giant component 96%, diameter 6.

**What replicates cleanly, across every dataset we could compute:** the giant component covers essentially the
whole graph (>99.4% everywhere, vs. Rome's 96%), the diameter is small (4-6, matching Rome's 6 almost exactly),
and clustering exceeds density by a wide margin everywhere (7x-42x, actually a *larger* gap than Rome's 3.7x).
This is a real, unforced small-world / highly-clustered signature, present in all five datasets we could build.

**What does NOT replicate as cleanly: the "heavy-tailed / scale-free" characterization specifically.** The
max-degree-to-mean-degree ratio for the four Gowalla US states is modest (7.6x-15.6x) compared to Istanbul's
176x, and an approximate log-log rank-vs-degree regression (Zipf-style, NOT a proper power-law MLE -- the
`powerlaw` package isn't installed) fits poorly for the four US states (r² = 0.54-0.64) but fits Istanbul well
(r² = 0.93):

| Dataset | log-log slope (rank vs degree) | r² |
|---|---:|---:|
| Alabama | -1.374 | 0.635 |
| Arizona | -1.389 | 0.592 |
| Florida | -1.382 | 0.542 |
| California | -1.396 | 0.551 |
| Istanbul | **-1.210** | **0.927** |

The Gowalla US co-visitation graphs are already so dense (avg degree in the hundreds to thousands, 5-9%
density) that they read more like uniformly dense small-world networks with a modest hub bump on top, rather
than a classic hub-and-spoke heavy-tailed topology. Istanbul (Massive-STEPS), which is architecturally closer
to the Rome paper's curated Foursquare tourism trace, is the one dataset here that is a genuinely clean
heavy-tailed match.

Our category taxonomy is coarse (7 buckets: Food, Shopping, Community, Travel, Entertainment, Outdoors,
Nightlife -- shared across all 6 datasets; verified single-valued per `placeid`, 0 conflicts in Alabama), much
coarser than Foursquare's fine-grained tree the Rome paper used, so we cannot say "train station" vs. "airport"
vs. "highway rest stop." But the qualitative match to Rome's "transit hubs and cultural landmarks dominate the
top of the degree distribution" is striking: Florida's and California's top-5 hubs are **all five** "Travel"
(the bucket that would hold airports, stations, and transit infrastructure), and Istanbul's hubs are dominated
by "Outdoors" (plausibly public squares/plazas acting as landmark gathering points -- the single highest-degree
Istanbul POI, id 4777, and #2, id 107, are both "Outdoors"), plus one "Travel" venue.

## 2. Texas: could not build the graph, reported rather than forced

Texas's raw co-occurrence matrix construction (the `M^T @ M` sparse matmul itself, before any thresholding was
even possible) **crashed the machine**: `/usr/bin/time -l` reported "command terminated abnormally" after
123.94s wall time, a maximum resident set size of ~7.7 GB, and a "peak memory footprint" of ~78.9 GB (i.e. it
was thrashing, not merely slow, on a 32 GB machine). What we do have without building the graph:

| | Texas |
|---|---:|
| check-ins | 4,089,892 |
| users | 38,644 |
| places | 160,938 |
| unique (user, place) pairs | 2,334,858 |
| max unique places visited by a single user | **27,153** (~17% of all Texas places) |
| sum of C(k,2) over users (upper bound on distinct co-visitation pairs) | 2.42 billion |

For comparison, California -- whose full graph **did** complete -- has a single-user maximum of 11,871 unique
places (~7% of California's places) and a sum-of-pairs of 973 million; California's `P` matrix ended up with
1.42 billion nonzero entries and needed ~6-14 GB RSS depending on the run. Texas's single most active user alone
(27,153 places) would contribute ~368 million pairwise combinations by itself. We deliberately did **not** work
around this by excluding that outlier user or any other ad hoc pre-filter, because that would silently change
the graph-construction method for Texas relative to every other state; per the task's own instructions, we
report the crash and the concrete numbers instead of forcing a result.

## 3. California: completed, but at the edge of a "light" budget

California's full graph **did** build successfully (matmul ~60-115s across runs, ~6-14 GB peak RSS) and its
connected-components/clustering-coefficient/degree-distribution numbers above are solid (connected_components
is exact and cheap regardless of density; the wedge-sampling clustering estimator is fast regardless of graph
size). Diameter/average-shortest-path is the one number we could not get comfortably: scipy's Dijkstra-based
`shortest_path` (used for every other dataset, including Florida at 215M edges) did not return **even a single
source's result** within 5.5 minutes on California's 708M-edge graph, so we switched to a hand-rolled O(V+E)
frontier BFS -- which still cost ~60-80 seconds *per source* at this density (a first-hop frontier that already
touches a large fraction of the 169,145 nodes, forcing a large `np.unique` per level). We used only **5** BFS
sources for California (vs. 300 for Alabama/Arizona/Istanbul, 60 for Florida); the two we watched live both
independently landed on diameter 5 with mean distance ~2.0-2.1, and the final 5-sample run gives diameter 4 /
mean 2.03 -- consistent with every other dataset's 4-6 / ~2.0-2.4, but this specific number should be treated as
a rough, small-n check, not a reliable estimate on its own. For the same reason (the high-activity segment would
be comparably dense to the full graph, since the same super-active users dominate both), we did **not** build
California's high/low-activity segment subgraphs -- Alabama, Arizona, Florida, and Istanbul already give a
clear, mutually consistent segmentation result (§5).

## 4. User activity distributions (the "length of stay" analogy)

Per-user total check-in count and active-day span, all users (including single-check-in users):

| Dataset | n users | single-check-in users | checkins p10/p25/p50/p75/p90/p99 | span-days p10/p25/p50/p75/p90/p99 |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Alabama | 3,858 | 1,088 (28.2%) | 1 / 1 / 3 / 12 / 55 / 465 | 0 / 0 / 2.4 / 80.9 / 260.4 / 544.5 |
| Arizona | 7,869 | 2,229 (28.3%) | 1 / 1 / 3 / 11 / 47 / 539 | 0 / 0 / 2.0 / 56.4 / 252.9 / 525.7 |
| Florida | 21,052 | 2,880 (13.7%) | 1 / 3 / 10 / 40 / 131 / 966 | 0 / 1.7 / 6.9 / 111.9 / 297.0 / 552.9 |
| California | 37,090 | 4,458 (12.0%) | 1 / 4 / 13 / 50 / 171 / 1,167 | 0 / 2.7 / 38.4 / 198.1 / 392.9 / 588.9 |
| Texas | 38,644 | 4,685 (12.1%) | 1 / 4 / 15 / 64 / 218 / 1,336 | 0 / 3.0 / 61.9 / 246.7 / 421.8 / 611.0 |
| Istanbul | 23,700 | **0 (0%)** | 6 / 8 / 13 / 25 / 47 / 148 | 37.2 / 92.3 / 186.7 / 309.1 / 462.0 / 2,171.3 |

**Honest read: this is an ordinary long-tail app-usage distribution, not an 18-month tourism-only trace, and we
should not oversell the tourist framing.** Gowalla is a historical, multi-year, general-purpose LBSN, not a
purpose-built tourism dataset. In every Gowalla state, 12-28% of users check in exactly once (contribute nothing
to the co-visitation graph); the *median* user has a span of 0-7 days and only 3-15 total check-ins, while the
*90th-99th percentile* user has a span of hundreds of days and hundreds-to-low-thousands of check-ins. That is
the classic shape of "most people try an app briefly, a small core keeps using it for years," not "most visitors
are here for 3 days, some residents stay 18 months." Istanbul (Massive-STEPS) is a different, evidently
pre-filtered/curated benchmark: it has **zero** single-check-in users and a much higher activity floor (even its
10th percentile user has 6 check-ins and a 37-day span), so it does not show the same raw long-tail shape at
all -- it looks like a dataset that was already restricted to reasonably active users.

Given this, we used **total check-in count**, not span-in-days, as the segmentation variable: span has heavy
ties at 0 (over a quarter of Alabama/Arizona users have span=0, i.e., every check-in on the same calendar day),
which makes percentile-based splitting on span degenerate, whereas total-checkin-count is a clean monotonic
long-tail and is also what mechanically drives graph density (more visited places -> more co-visitation edges),
making it the more meaningful lever for testing whether topology tracks activity level.

## 5. Segmentation: high-activity vs. low-activity co-visitation subgraphs

Top 10% of users by total check-in count = high-activity segment. A comparably-sized (k-matched) segment of the
**lowest**-activity users among **multi-check-in (>=2) users only** (single-check-in users contribute zero
edges) = low-activity segment. Same graph-construction method as §1, restricted to each segment's own
check-ins.

**This replicates the Rome paper's headline segmentation finding cleanly at Alabama, Arizona, and Florida**: the
high-activity segment's co-visitation subgraph is dense, fully connected (giant component = 100.0% at all
three), and highly clustered (C 0.57-0.73); the low-activity segment's subgraph is sparse, badly fragmented
(giant component 12-44%, hundreds of separate tiny components), and has essentially no clustering (C
0.005-0.026). The clustering-coefficient gap between high and low is dramatic: 137x at Alabama, 110x at Arizona,
22x at Florida. This is the same qualitative pattern as Rome's long-stay (C=0.71, density=0.43, avg deg=1,312,
diameter=4) vs. short-stay (C=0.17, density=0.01, avg deg=19, diameter=7) split, and in our data the contrast is
if anything *starker* on the clustering axis (though our "low" segment's absolute numbers are far sparser than
Rome's short-stay segment, because our low segment is literally the 2-3-checkin floor of a multi-year dataset,
not a bounded 3-day visit).

**Istanbul shows the same direction but a much weaker contrast**, and we think we know why: Istanbul's "low"
segment users still have a floor of 6 check-ins (vs. 2 for the Gowalla states, since Istanbul has no
single-check-in users to begin with, see §4), so Istanbul's low-activity segment is not nearly as sparse a
starting point. Its giant component stays 98.4% connected even in the "low" segment, and the clustering gap is
only ~1.3x, not two orders of magnitude. We read this as a real, honest effect of Istanbul being a pre-filtered
benchmark rather than a raw historical scrape, not a failure to replicate.

We did not run this split for Texas (whose full graph we could not build at all) or California (§3).

## 6. Verdict

**Would this experiment's findings credibly support a sentence like "consistent with recent network-science
analyses of tourist check-in data, our own check-in graphs also show hub-dominated, heavy-tailed structure"?**

Split into what is and is not supportable:

- **Well supported, across every dataset we could build (5 of 6):** the co-visitation graph is dominated by a
  giant connected component (>99.4%, vs. Rome's 96%), is small-world (diameter 4-6, matching Rome's 6), and is
  far more clustered than its density would predict (C exceeds density by 7x-42x, vs. Rome's 3.7x). Real hub
  POIs exist everywhere, and at Florida/California they are *exclusively* the "Travel" category (the closest our
  coarse taxonomy gets to Rome's "transit hubs"); at Istanbul they are "Outdoors" (plausible landmark
  squares/plazas). A sentence scoped to **"hub-dominated, small-world, densely clustered structure"** is
  honestly supportable and not cherry-picked.
- **Well supported, but only at Alabama/Arizona/Florida (not yet checked at California/Texas):** segmenting
  users by activity intensity (our closest tractable analogue to Rome's length-of-stay split) reproduces Rome's
  finding that the busier segment's network is dense/cohesive/fully-connected while the quieter segment's
  network is sparse/fragmented, with a two-order-of-magnitude clustering gap. This is a genuine, non-forced
  structural echo, though our "low-activity" segment means literally 2-3 total lifetime check-ins, not a
  bounded temporal visit -- the paper sentence should say "activity-intensity segmentation," not literally
  "length of stay" or "tourists," if it wants to stay honest about Gowalla's non-tourism nature.
- **Weaker / mixed, and we would not lean on it:** the specific **"heavy-tailed / scale-free degree
  distribution"** framing is a clean match only at Istanbul (max/mean degree ratio 176x, rank-degree log-log
  r²=0.93). The four Gowalla US states are already so densely connected (5-9% density, avg degree in the
  hundreds to thousands) that their degree distributions are only moderately skewed (max/mean 7.6x-15.6x,
  r²=0.54-0.64) -- a real hub effect, but not the cleanly heavy-tailed shape the Rome paper reports for Rome's
  sparser (14.5% density but far fewer, more spread-out) Foursquare graph. If the paper's sentence specifically
  claims "heavy-tailed" or "scale-free," it should either scope that claim to Istanbul, or soften it to "hub
  effects present but our graphs are considerably denser and less classically heavy-tailed than the tourist
  benchmark," rather than claiming a blanket match across all datasets.
- **Not attempted / not claimable:** Texas (graph construction itself was intractable on this machine) and
  California's segmentation split and reliable diameter (both explicitly skipped/approximated, see §§2-3).

**Overall**: a paper sentence is defensible if scoped to the giant-component/small-world/clustering-vs-density
result (strong, uniform, unforced across 5/6 datasets) and, separately, to the activity-segmentation topology
split (strong at 3/6, directionally present at a 4th). A blanket "heavy-tailed" claim across all our data would
be overreaching; that specific claim is genuinely well supported only for Istanbul.
